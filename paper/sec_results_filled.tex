\section{Results}

\subsection{The corpora overlap, and exact matching does not see it}

Table~\ref{tab:overlap} and Fig.~\ref{fig:overlap} show what the nine sources
share. Compared exactly as a model reads them, SpamAssassin and Kaggle have
2 emails in common. With whitespace removed they have 3{,}850, and with
near-duplicate matching 5{,}032, which is 87\% of SpamAssassin. Kaggle
also holds 91\% of Ling-Spam and 36\% of Enron, and 93\% of
the Kaggle corpus has a near copy in some other corpus. The Kaggle ``dataset''
is in large part three older corpora put together with the formatting changed
on the way. CEAS-08, TREC-07, Nazario and E-PhishLLM share almost nothing with
any other source, which gives us a control group for the next experiment.

The result does not depend on where we put the threshold
(Table~\ref{tab:within}): at a Jaccard of 0.9 the Kaggle share is still
86\%. The matcher agrees with brute force on the hard cases: on 400
SpamAssassin emails with no whitespace-exact match in Kaggle, the pipeline has
precision 1.00 and recall 1.00 against an exhaustive search, and
62\% of those hard cases really do have a near copy in Kaggle. The
choice of how much of each email to compare does not matter either: repeating
the study over the first 5{,}000 characters instead of 2{,}000 moves the
SpamAssassin figure from 86.7\% to 87.1\%.

Table~\ref{tab:within} also shows that the problem starts inside each corpus.
Between 10\% and 29\% of a corpus has a near duplicate elsewhere in the
same corpus, and 9 to 19\% of the evaluation emails have a near copy
in the part of their own corpus that a model trains on. Ordinary in-corpus
evaluation is therefore already optimistic before any cross-corpus question is
asked.

\subsection{Leaked emails inflate cross-corpus scores}

Table~\ref{tab:pairwise} repeats the single-corpus transfer test. Training on
Kaggle and testing on SpamAssassin looks almost perfect at F1 0.966, and
falls to 0.672 when the Kaggle training emails that also appear in
SpamAssassin are removed. The pair that shares nothing, CEAS-08 to TREC-07,
does not move at all.

Table~\ref{tab:inflation} shows the same effect under leave-one-corpus-out,
together with the control. Removing leaked emails costs logistic regression
12.1 points on Ling-Spam and 10.8 on SpamAssassin, while CEAS-08 and
TREC-07, which share almost nothing with the others, move by 0.1 points or
less. Removing the same number of training emails at random instead changes
almost nothing: the random setting stays within 0.4 points of the raw
setting on every corpus. So the drop is caused by which emails are removed, not
by how many.

Fig.~\ref{fig:incorpus} puts the settings side by side. The usual in-corpus
split gives 0.930 to 0.986. Honest leave-one-corpus-out gives 0.832 to
0.927. The in-corpus number overstates what the model will do on mail from a
new source by 4 to 15 points.

\subsection{What the benchmarks actually contain}
\label{sec:labels}

Before comparing models it is worth knowing what the positive class is.
Table~\ref{tab:labels} gives the answer: across the six corpora only
15 to 25\% of the positive evaluation emails are phishing. The rest is
bulk advertising. The two annotators agree on 86\% of the 300 doubly
annotated emails (Cohen's $\kappa$ 0.35, held down by the strong imbalance
between the two classes).

This matters for how results in this field should be read. A model that scores
well on these corpora is mostly proving that it can find spam. Every model we
tested detects the phishing subset slightly better than the spam subset, by
1 to 14 points, so the ranking does not change, but the
headline numbers describe a different task from the one the papers name.

\textit{Small open LLMs are competitive on unseen corpora.} Zero-shot
Qwen-2.5-7B reaches a mean F1 of 0.928 over the six held-out corpora,
above TF-IDF with logistic regression (0.899) and the fine-tuned DistilBERT
(0.891), and it raises the fewest false alarms of the open models at
3\% of legitimate mail. The paired bootstrap puts the difference to
logistic regression at +0.029 (interval +0.016 to +0.044), and an exact McNemar
test on the pooled 1{,}800 emails gives $p=5e-5$. The jackknife column is a
reminder of how much one corpus can move the average.

\textit{Published detectors show the contamination from the other side.}
The released detectors behave as contamination predicts. BERT-phishing reaches 0.962 on our legacy corpora and 0.470 on AI-written phishing; Phishsense-1B reaches 0.947 on our legacy corpora and 0.839 on AI-written phishing; ModernBERT-phishing reaches 0.892 on our legacy corpora and 0.149 on AI-written phishing. BERT-phishing has the highest unseen-corpus score in the whole table (0.962), above every LLM we ran, which is what a leaderboard built on these corpora would report. Its model card names the same Kaggle lineage we test on, so that column is partly a memory test, and the AI-written column is where the difference shows. The one exception is the LoRA-tuned causal model, which transfers much better than the two encoder classifiers, so the failure is not a property of released models as such. We also checked that this is not simply the decision threshold. The ModernBERT card recommends 0.37 rather than the default. On AI-written phishing its recall goes from 0.09 at the default to 0.11 at the recommended threshold and 0.22 at 0.2, while the false alarm rate on the same set moves from 8\% to 33\%. Lowering the threshold trades one failure for the other rather than removing it.

\textit{A current commercial model is still ahead.} Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite
reaches 0.960 on unseen corpora with 1.7\% false alarms and 0.950 on
AI-written phishing, better than every open model we ran on all three axes, at
0.080 dollars per 1{,}000 emails. The honest summary for a small
organisation is not that open models win, but that they cost less and lose
some accuracy, and the table says how much of each.

\textit{The best in-corpus model is among the worst on new attacks.}
DistilBERT has the highest in-corpus F1 in the table (0.975) and one of the
lowest scores on AI-written phishing (0.540). Ranking by the usual measure
would pick it first. It is also the most truncated model in our setup at 128
word pieces, which is a second reason to read that row carefully.

\textit{Format compliance is part of capability.} Phi-4 ignored the one-word
instruction on 37\% of emails, starting an explanation that the token
limit then cut off, and those answers count as missed detections. Given room to
answer it behaves quite differently (unseen-corpus F1 0.754 against 0.727, with 4\% unparsed instead of 37\%), which is a warning
about how much of a published LLM score can depend on output formatting rather
than detection.

\subsection{AI-written phishing, and a token that looked like a shortcut}

E-PhishGen writes phishing links as the literal placeholder
\texttt{<<link>>}. In our English evaluation subset 94 of the 150 phishing
emails contain such a placeholder and 0 of the 150 legitimate ones do. A
detector could react to the token instead of to the message, which would make
our AI-phishing numbers meaningless, so we tested it two ways.

The first test is the one that comes to mind: split the phishing emails by
whether they carry a placeholder and compare recall. This gives a large gap,
16 points on average, and it is misleading. The two halves are different
emails: the ones with a placeholder are link-based lures, the ones without are
business email compromise and similar text-only attacks, which are harder for
any detector.

The second test is paired and is the one that answers the question. We rebuild
the same 300 emails with the placeholder removed, and with it replaced by a
plausible URL, and compare each model's verdict on the same email before and
after. The mean change in recall is 0.9 points, and the largest for any
model is 9.6 points. Table~\ref{tab:aiphish} shows the F1 on all three versions:
they differ by little, and for several models the sanitised version scores
slightly higher.

So the token is not what the detectors were keying on, and the AI-phishing
results stand. We report it because the naive test would have supported the
opposite conclusion, and because anyone using this corpus should know the
token is there. The same caution applies to the legitimate half of the corpus,
which is synthetic as well: a detector that flags synthetic business mail is
being tested on two shifts at once.

On the Italian and German parts, prompted in English, the better models do not
lose much (Gemini 0.966, Gemma 0.948 on Italian) while the smaller
ones move more, so language transfer depends on the model rather than being a
uniform penalty.

\subsection{Where few-shot examples come from matters}

Four examples drawn from the other legacy corpora raise Qwen-2.5-7B from
0.928 to 0.937 on the held-out corpora, which the paired
bootstrap cannot separate from zero (difference +0.009, interval -0.001 to +0.018), and lower its AI-phishing F1
from 0.819 to 0.598, which is significant (McNemar $p=2e-12$). Replacing
those examples with AI-written ones, and changing nothing else, recovers the
AI-phishing score to 0.723 at the price of a higher false alarm rate
(10\% against 3\%). A mixed prompt lands between the two
(0.673). The same pattern holds for Llama-3.2-3B.

The lesson is not that few-shot prompting is bad. It is that the examples act
as a description of what phishing looks like, so examples from 2002 to 2008
teach a model to expect mail from that era.

\subsection{Operating points: base rate and two-stage detectors}

Our evaluation sets are balanced, real mailboxes are not.
Table~\ref{tab:baserate} recomputes precision at a 5\% phishing rate, keeping
the measured recall and false alarm rate. The ranking changes: Gemma-3-12B,
which is the best open model on AI-written phishing, drops to F1 0.289
because it would flag 239 legitimate emails in every thousand, while
Qwen stays at 0.725 with 28.

Table~\ref{tab:cascade} builds two-stage detectors from models we have already
measured, with no new calls. Sending only the mail that TF-IDF flags to
Qwen-2.5-7B gives F1 0.911 with 1.1\% false alarms at
0.0161 dollars per 1{,}000 emails, which is half the cost of running Qwen
on everything and a lower false alarm rate than either model alone. In the
other direction, letting Gemma see the mail that Qwen cleared raises recall on
AI-written phishing to 0.987 at the price of 25\% false alarms,
which is the shape of a quarantine queue rather than a blocker.

\subsection{Explanations}

On 100 emails, both small models always produced a reason when asked.
A judge model found the reason grounded in the email for 91\% of
Qwen's answers and 92\% of Gemma's. The interesting part is what
happens when the verdict is wrong: then only 53\% of the reasons are
grounded, that is, a wrong verdict tends to come with an invented
justification. Reading a sample by hand agrees with the judge. This supports
the concern of Kuikel et al.~\cite{kuikel} that accuracy and explanation
quality are different things, and it argues against showing a model's reason to
an end user without checking the verdict first.

\subsection{Cost}

Zero-shot classification cost between 0.009 and 0.033 US dollars
per 1{,}000 emails for the open models and 0.080 for
Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite, measured from the charge reported for every call on
1{,}500-character emails. TF-IDF needs 0.09 to 0.40 CPU seconds per
1{,}000 emails for inference and no API. DistilBERT needs 7 minutes of
laptop CPU time per training fold and 22 seconds per 1{,}000 emails. The
whole study, 40{,}498 model calls including the ones we discarded, cost
1.58 US dollars.
